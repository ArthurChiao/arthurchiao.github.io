# 1 Introduction

AI is changing not only how software gets built, but how direction travels
through a company. Discussion often focuses on faster execution. A less discussed
change is happening upstream: a passing intuition can now become a detailed
proposal within minutes, complete with architecture diagrams, milestones,
interfaces, and risk lists.

When its author has agenda-setting power, that output may arrive downstream not
as one candidate among many, but as a direction to review and execute. This is
not because managers have suddenly become arbitrary or technically incompetent.
Top-down direction existed long before AI. What changed is the practical limit
on how quickly the upstream can turn ideas into apparently finished plans. The
downstream's capacity to test assumptions, reconcile dependencies, and carry
consequences remains finite.

One side can therefore generate directions at near-zero marginal cost; the other
must spend scarce professional attention digesting, falsifying, and making them
work. The danger is not merely more work. <strong><mark>Processing instructions
begins to displace exercising judgment.</mark></strong>

# 2 A Mirror: AI-Generated Mathematics

In October 2026, OpenAI published 719 mathematical manuscripts generated by its
internal models, grouped into 372 result families, and framed the release as
"sharing AI's progress in mathematics". The controversy was not only whether
the results were true. It was also who would verify, understand, and explain
them: a finite human mathematical community. Some mathematicians objected that
checking this volume of unsolicited output would turn them into unpaid labor
confirming or refuting OpenAI's claims of progress.

The episode exposes one structure with unusual clarity:
when an upstream can produce apparently finished results cheaply and
at scale, while verification and integration still depend on scarce human
attention, the output itself can become a burden on the
downstream.

Mathematical proof and product delivery are not the same, but both reveal a
<strong><mark><code>generation–verification asymmetry</code></mark></strong>:
one side cheaply creates candidates for
attention; the other spends expert time deciding which deserve to be taken
seriously and turning answers into shared knowledge.

Inside a company, the asymmetry is more consequential. A mathematical community
can publicly decline to validate a claim or reject the agenda imposed on it.
Employees receiving a proposal through a chain of authority usually cannot.
They must investigate it, explain why it will or will not work, and often execute
it while remaining accountable for the result.

# 3 When the Upstream Gains Near-Infinite Expressive Power

## 3.1 From Intuition to Apparently Complete Plan

Organizations have always had an upstream and a downstream. Managers set goals,
resources, and priorities; front-line and middle-layer teams translated them
into workable plans using local knowledge. The process was often slow and
bureaucratic, but it gave reality a chance to enter before a direction hardened
into an instruction.

AI weakens that boundary. An intuition can be packaged as a coherent plan
without first passing through people who know the systems, dependencies, and
history involved. The plan may be useful. The structural temptation comes from
its appearance: when polished output is cheap, it is easy to assume that problem
definition, constraint discovery, and tradeoff analysis have already been done.
Anyone who objects must then explain why a complete-looking proposal will not
survive contact with reality — and overcome both its polish and the authority
behind it.

## 3.2 The Upstream Is Not One Person

In a medium-sized or large enterprise, the upstream is rarely a single leader.
AI gives many people the ability to inject detailed demands into the same
execution system at once.

Requests used to be partly filtered by the effort required to formulate and
coordinate them. As those filters weaken, requests arrive already packaged as
plausible plans, but may never have been merged, prioritized, or made mutually
consistent. Each can look reasonable in isolation while the same downstream
teams must reconcile all of them in practice. The result is a
<strong><mark>demand explosion from uncoordinated sources</mark></strong>, all
drawing on the same finite capacity for attention, judgment, and execution.

## 3.3 Generation Is Visible; Digestion Is Invisible

The upstream generates plans. The downstream must assess them, reconcile them
with existing commitments, fill in omissions, resolve conflicts, and own the
consequences after launch.

Generation is visible and easy to count: proposals written, tasks created,
decisions announced. Digestion is mostly invisible: tracing dependencies,
discovering conflicting directions, explaining why a reasonable-looking plan is
not feasible, or repairing the gaps between an instruction and reality. The
proposal gets credited; the work required to make it real — or prove that it
should not be made real — is mistaken for routine execution.

# 4 From Professional Judgment to Instruction Processing

## 4.1 Judgment Is Learned Through Participation

Professional judgment develops through a repeated loop: forming a view, testing
it against reality, receiving feedback, and revising it. In a functioning
division of labor, execution teams helped define problems, surface constraints,
weigh tradeoffs, propose alternatives, and see the consequences of decisions.
That participation gave people room not only to use judgment, but to develop it.

When more of the solution arrives from above already specified, the execution
team remains busy, but its work changes. It spends less time exploring the
problem and shaping a solution, and more time interpreting, reconciling, and
working around instructions.

## 4.2 Thinking Is Displaced

This does not mean the execution layer stops thinking. <strong><mark>Its
thinking is displaced.</mark></strong>

Less thought goes into asking what problem should be solved, forming
alternatives, and testing assumptions before a direction is chosen. More goes
into deciphering detailed demands, filling in omissions, proving or disproving
feasibility, and coping with consequences. The organization still consumes
professional judgment, but increasingly uses it to process instructions rather
than influence direction. The upstream owns both direction and solution; the
downstream remains accountable for making that solution work.

## 4.3 What the Old Boundaries Protected

The old boundaries were not inherently good. They produced delay, bureaucracy,
and misunderstanding, and did not guarantee sound judgment. But in a functioning
organization, they gave reality multiple points of entry before a direction
hardened into an instruction. They protected:

- **Local knowledge**: front-line teams know the undocumented dependencies,
  and historical baggage that surface only in practice.
- **The ability to dissent**: when those proposing a plan and bearing its
  consequences differ, "this does not hold up" must be allowed to change what
  happens next.
- **The next layer of judgment**: people learn to carry broader responsibility by
  defining problems, proposing solutions, and observing consequences.

The danger is not AI's participation in proposal generation. It is that AI
lets an organization bypass the participation through which local knowledge,
dissent, and future judgment enter the system. Remove people from that loop long
enough, and they lose the conditions under which professional judgment
develops.

# 5 Two Organizational Fables

AI is often described as a flattening force: teams shrink, individuals become
more capable, and companies begin to resemble an OPC — a one-person company in
which one person uses AI to cover much of the operating and delivery work. That
is one real possibility, especially for small companies. It is not the only one.

The same technology can produce two opposite forms: one person
with many capabilities, or one brain with many limbs. The
difference is whether generation, verification, judgment, and consequences
remain within a closed loop.

## 5.1 One Person with Many Capabilities

An OPC or very small team can work when outcomes can be verified quickly, errors
are reversible and contained, and the person choosing a direction remains close
to its execution and consequences. AI can then help one person explore, choose,
build, test, and change course.

The point is not that one person does everything or always decides well. It is
that the person opening a loop is also responsible for closing it. Cheap
generation does not impose expensive verification on a separate group with less
power to reject the work. AI can therefore expand individual autonomy rather
than strip judgment from others.

## 5.2 One Brain with Many Limbs

That pattern does not scale cleanly to a medium-sized or large enterprise.
Verification is often delayed and context-dependent; errors cross teams,
systems, customers, and long time horizons; and no small upstream can absorb all
the consequences of the directions it generates.

If the upstream nevertheless uses AI to centralize both direction and solution,
the rest of the organization continues executing, verifying, integrating, and
patching — with less room to define problems, propose alternatives, or change
direction. The enterprise drifts from many judgment nodes toward one brain with
many limbs.

This drift is a risk, not a fate. It takes hold where the upstream controls the
agenda, feedback is slow, and dissent cannot change direction. Where verification
is quick, errors are cheap, and teams retain that power, AI can strengthen a
distributed judgment network instead.

In a small company, AI can help one person close more of the loop. In a
medium-sized or large enterprise, it can allow a small upstream to open far more
loops than the rest of the organization can meaningfully close.

# 6 How a Generative Boom May Weaken Innovation

The question here is not whether AI can innovate. It is where an enterprise's
scarce capacity for attention, dissent, experimentation, and learning goes when
candidate answers become extremely cheap.

## 6.1 Absorb Cognitive Capacity

Expert attention is finite. Time spent assessing and reconciling an expanding
pile of upstream proposals is time not spent noticing unarticulated problems,
pursuing long-horizon experiments, or learning deeply from failure.

This can still look productive: more proposals are reviewed, more tasks are
created, and more visible output moves through the system. But much of the
organization's best judgment is being consumed by deciding whether generated
candidates can be made to work.

## 6.2 Move the Agenda from Questions to Answers

As the upstream keeps emitting answers worth discussing, it becomes harder for
the downstream to raise what should be discussed in the first place. Teams end
up verifying "can this be built?" rather than asking "is this worth building?"
Once the organization is saturated with answers from above, there is less room
for questions to emerge from below.

AI may generate many architectures, roadmaps, and product ideas, yet the agenda
can still originate from a narrow part of the organization. A distributed
judgment network matters not only because it checks more answers, but because
people in different positions notice different problems.

## 6.3 Weaken the Production of Future Judgment

Innovation depends on an organization's ability to keep developing people who
can define problems, detect hidden constraints, form independent views, design
experiments, and own consequences. Those abilities grow through participation.

If execution teams spend years processing plans formed elsewhere, they may
become highly effective at delivery while receiving fewer opportunities to
practice higher-level judgment. The enterprise loses not only ideas that might
have emerged today, but the next generation capable of setting sound direction
tomorrow.

This is how a generative boom becomes a <strong><mark>pseudo-innovation
boom</mark></strong>: more proposals, more visible activity, and less discovery.
The company may look faster while consuming the human conditions that make
sustained innovation possible.

# 7 Closing

The mathematics case reveals a general imbalance: when generation approaches
zero cost while verification and digestion remain expensive, output becomes
abundant faster than a system can turn it into knowledge.

Inside a medium-sized or large enterprise, hierarchy turns this imbalance into
a shift of judgment: upstream ideas arrive as directions, consuming downstream
thought in making them work rather than defining problems or alternatives. The
danger is not AI replacing thought, but concentrating direction while converting
distributed judgment into instruction-processing capacity. The enterprise may
move faster, yet become less able to understand reality, correct itself, and
develop future innovators.
